The Digital Nutrition Label: Why the EU Should Force Platforms to Tell You What They're Feeding You

The EU mandates ingredient lists on yogurt and emission stats on cars. But the algorithmic services shaping the information diet of 450 million Europeans have zero mandatory per-user behavioral disclosure. A proposal for standardized transparency APIs.

GK
Gergely K.
March 2026 · 10 min read

The Gap

Consider what the European Union already mandates. Food products carry detailed ingredient lists, allergen warnings, and calorie counts per serving. Cars display fuel consumption, CO₂ emissions, and safety ratings. Cosmetics list every chemical compound. Tobacco packaging shows health warnings covering most of the surface. Financial products come with standardized risk disclosures.

Now consider what the EU requires algorithmic services to tell you — the individual user — about how they specifically affect your behavior. The answer: nothing. Zero mandatory per-user behavioral data endpoints exist.

The Transparency Gap
93% of EU minors use social media
Children and adolescents are the most algorithmically influenced demographic, with the least capacity to understand the manipulation. Yet there is no "nutrition label" for their digital diet.

The Proposal

The Digital Nutrition Label is not a new regulatory framework. It is the logical next step in an existing trajectory — extending the principle of "tell the regulator" (GDPR, DSA) to "tell the user." Concretely, it would require any algorithmic service above a user threshold to expose a standardized API endpoint returning per-user behavioral data in a common format.

What would that data look like? A weekly "nutrition report" showing: total engagement time and its distribution across content types; estimated ad revenue generated from your attention; number and category of purchase decisions influenced by recommendations; preference drift — how your content profile has shifted over the reporting period; and a comparison to population baselines so you can see whether your patterns are typical or outlier.

The Regulatory Trajectory

This proposal isn't emerging from nowhere. It sits at the end of a clear legislative arc. GDPR (2018) established the right to access your personal data — but platforms return raw data dumps, not behavioral insight. The DSA (2022) required platforms to explain why content is recommended — but to the regulator, not the user, and not with behavioral impact data. The AI Act (2024) classifies high-risk AI systems — but recommendation algorithms largely escaped the high-risk category. The Digital Fairness Act (expected Q4 2026) is the natural vehicle for mandating user-facing behavioral transparency.

Why Transparency Alone Might Work

A common objection: knowing your data won't change behavior. But this misunderstands the mechanism. Calorie labels didn't make individuals eat less — they made companies reformulate products to avoid embarrassing numbers on the label. Similarly, a Digital Nutrition Label wouldn't primarily change user behavior. It would change platform behavior, because no company wants a standardized API broadcasting "we made €14.20 from this user's attention this week and shifted their political preferences 12% rightward."

The children protection angle makes this politically irresistible across party lines.

What's Next

The initiative is live at digitalnutritionlabel.eu. The roadmap: concept paper and landing page (done), followed by academic partnership building with HCI and algorithmic auditing research groups, then a Horizon Europe or Digital Europe Programme consortium application targeting coordinator or work package lead roles. The Digital Fairness Act preparation phase creates a perfect window to inject the "mandatory behavioral transparency API" concept into the legislative discussion.
