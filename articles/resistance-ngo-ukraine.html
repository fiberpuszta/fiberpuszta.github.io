When Real Corruption Meets Geopolitical Narrative: The RESISTANCE NGO Case

Maria Barabash's NGO exposed real Ukrainian wartime procurement scandals — tenders were actually cancelled. But her pro-Trump positioning and amplification by Russian-aligned media raise a harder question: can genuine anti-corruption work simultaneously serve foreign interests?

GK
Gergely K.
March 2026 · 7 min read

The Background Check

Maria Barabash, born January 25, 1985 in Kyiv. Education at Taras Shevchenko National University confirmed: Faculty of Philosophy (2002–2008), second degree in law (2013). Programs at the Aspen Institute Kyiv (2016) and the National University of Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy (2021). The credentials check out.

Her career has two phases. First, investment consulting: she founded A7 Group in 2010, organized over 100 business conferences globally, ran the first Ukrainian Defence and Security Investment Forum in 2016, and served as vice president of CFA Society Ukraine (2011–2015). Second, activism: she headed the office of the Reform Council under Saakashvili, then pivoted to independent anti-corruption work.

The NGO

RESISTANCE was founded April 19, 2024, growing from the "Money for the Armed Forces" campaign. By December 2024: 293 members from 12 countries. Their track record involves exposing wartime procurement irregularities: vegetable cutters for bomb shelters at ~131,000 UAH each, leather drums for shelters at 900,000 UAH total, a 300-million-UAH tender for tennis court renovations during wartime, and a 5-million-UAH tender linked to Rostyslav Shurma, a deputy head of the President's Office — after which the tender was cancelled.

These are real findings. Tenders were actually cancelled after her disclosures. You don't get that outcome from fabricated claims.

The Red Flags

Political positioning: She describes herself as one of few Ukrainian activists who supports Trump and Republicans in Ukraine. This is unusual in the Ukrainian activist space and signals alignment with specific US political networks.

Media ecosystem: Most English-language coverage of her Maidan rally activities comes from "Pravda EN" (news-pravda.com) — a Russian-aligned aggregator, not to be confused with the legitimate Ukrainska Pravda. The surrounding content is consistently pro-Russian editorial.

Rhetoric: Her Maidan rally described the situation in Pokrovsk as an "endless mass grave" — framing that goes beyond anti-corruption into territory that directly undermines Ukrainian military morale and legitimacy.

The Hardest Question

Genuine anti-corruption work and serving external political interests aren't mutually exclusive. In fact, the most effective influence operations weaponize real information rather than fabricating it. The playbook: find real dirt (easy in wartime Ukraine), package it, amplify through sympathetic channels, let it serve someone else's narrative.

"The useful idiot model is far more common than the paid agent model. Russia doesn't need to pay someone who genuinely believes Zelenskyy's circle is corrupt and channels that belief through media that serves Russian narratives."

The most likely explanation is narrative capture. People who fight real corruption develop a worldview where the government is the enemy — and once that frame locks in, anything confirming it gets a pass on fact-checking. There's also a structural feedback loop: once you're amplified by certain media ecosystems, your audience shifts, your incentives shift, and the anti-corruption work becomes inseparable from a political agenda, even if that wasn't the original intention.
