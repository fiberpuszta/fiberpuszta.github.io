AI Will Boost Productivity in the Global North — and Make Inequality Worse Everywhere Else

Robot doctors won't be running the show in rural Hungary anytime soon. AI deployment requires capital that concentrates in the Global North. The developer as a profession is ending — but solving problems across domains will remain human work.

GK
Gergely K.
March 2026 · 4 min read

The robot doctors in rural Hungary example is spot on. This predicts one thing: AI will boost productivity further in the Global North, where they have enough money to deploy it, making the differences between and within societies even bigger. Practically, that's it.

The only exception is maybe software development, which is more location-agnostic and can be done remotely. As a software developer, I think the coding part itself will soon become a digital competence very similar to Word or Excel — something that is a must whatever job you have. So yes, the developer as a profession is over soon, but solving problems across various domains will stay with us and requires human interaction.

"Job of the future: Professional with a social worker background who can write mobile applications and perform data analytics with AI to support a robot doctor service launch in rural Hungary."

The Structural Point

AI deployment is a capital-intensive activity. Training large models costs hundreds of millions. Deploying inference infrastructure costs millions. Even using API services requires reliable internet, electricity, and organizations with budgets for software tooling. Every one of these requirements correlates with existing wealth.

This means AI follows the same pattern as every previous wave of productive technology: it amplifies existing advantages. Countries, regions, and companies that are already productive become more productive. Those that aren't fall further behind — not because they're doing anything wrong, but because the gap in capability widens automatically.

The Paradigm Shift Nobody Wants to Discuss

And the hardest paradigm shift is how we value work on a societal level. Maybe work won't be the main role of humans in society anymore. We could pay people for other things: for learning, for keeping themselves healthy (to reduce healthcare system load), for doing useful social activities, for writing blogs. And the robots do the factory work, which fits them better anyway.

This isn't utopian dreaming — it's a practical question about social contracts. If AI generates enough surplus to sustain a society, the distribution question becomes political rather than economic. Who decides who gets what? The answer to that question determines whether AI becomes the great equalizer or the great concentrator.
