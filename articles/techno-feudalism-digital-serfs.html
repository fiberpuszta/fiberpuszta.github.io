The New Feudalism: Are We Becoming Digital Serfs?

Goods and power were concentrated in the 17th–18th centuries, then decentralized, and are now reconcentrating around energy, compute, and materials. The Hungarian zsellér may be the best historical analogy for a future digitally dependent population.

GK
Gergely K.
March 2026 · 8 min read

The Cycle

There is a pattern in the distribution of productive resources that spans centuries. In the 17th and 18th centuries, goods and power were highly concentrated — land, mills, waterways, skilled labor all controlled by a small elite. The industrial revolution and its political aftershocks decentralized access: mass production, mass education, mass political participation. For roughly two centuries, the trend line pointed toward broader distribution.

That trend is reversing. Control over the three inputs that matter most for the next century — energy, compute, and raw materials — is reconcentrating in fewer hands than at any point since the pre-industrial era. The difference is that the new concentration isn't geographic. It's infrastructural.

The Hungarian Analogy

The zsellér was the landless peasant of the Hungarian feudal system. Not a slave — technically free. Could sell labor, could move (sometimes). But without land, every function of life required someone else's permission and someone else's infrastructure. Housing, food production, social standing — all dependent on the landowner's continued tolerance.

The digital equivalent is emerging. You can "freely" run a business — on Amazon's cloud. You can "freely" reach your audience — through Meta's algorithm. You can "freely" develop software — using AI tools whose pricing and access policies change without notice. The zsellér could leave the estate. But where would they go?

"We don't just use cars to go farther — we restructure cities so walking becomes impractical. We don't just use AI to think faster — we restructure work so thinking without AI becomes uncompetitive."

The Academic Landscape

Yanis Varoufakis formalized this intuition in his techno-feudalism framework: cloud capital as the new landlordism. Platform owners don't just sell to you — they rent you access to the marketplace itself. Acemoglu and Johnson, in Power and Progress, make a complementary argument: technology doesn't automatically create shared prosperity. The direction of innovation depends on who controls it. When AI development is controlled by a handful of companies, the gains accrue to capital, not labor.

The IMF's 2024 research quantified the risk: AI is likely to affect 40% of jobs globally, but in advanced economies the figure is closer to 60%. Half of those affected could see productivity gains; the other half face displacement with no clear path to equivalent work.

The Counterargument

David Autor offers the strongest optimistic case: AI could actually de-concentrate expertise by making specialist knowledge accessible to generalists. A nurse with AI tools can do work that previously required a specialist physician. A small-town lawyer can compete with a corporate firm. If this plays out, AI becomes democratizing rather than feudalizing.

The question is whether Autor's scenario or Varoufakis's scenario wins. The answer likely depends on regulation, open-source development, and whether governments treat AI infrastructure as a public utility or a private monopoly. That's a political choice, not a technological inevitability.
