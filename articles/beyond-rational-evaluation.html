Beyond Rational Evaluation: How Modern Politics Actually Works

Politics is no longer about evaluating politicians based on their plans and casting votes accordingly. It follows a completely different dynamic — one driven by emotion, fear, belonging, and the need for protection from the complexity of the world.

GK
Gergely K.
March 2026 · 5 min read

Politics is not about watching the news, evaluating politicians based on their values, plans, achievements, or statements, and then casting votes accordingly. It follows a completely different dynamic now — one driven by emotion, fear, anger, a sense of belonging, even entertainment, and a great deal of entirely illogical forces. Scandals sometimes serve the very person acting scandalously, simply by dominating public discourse or keeping the focus away from poor economic decisions.

The News Is a Different Thing Now

Even the news itself is a completely different phenomenon than it used to be. It no longer serves the purpose of informing us about what happens around us through credible mainstream media. Instead, it is about finding the narrative that best fits one's echo chamber and is the most entertaining.

Consider a concrete example: an interview where a political leader makes statements that are, by any conventional standard, damaging. This interview represents a credible primary source. But for many people, they would dismiss it as fake news. They would never believe that their beloved president said such things — or they would insist he meant it differently, or that it is part of a bigger plan.

"Anything, really, that protects them from the complexity of the world and the hard work of critical thinking."

The Structural Problem

This is not simply a matter of stupidity or laziness. The information environment has been restructured so that critical thinking is actively penalized. Following one source carefully, checking claims, reading primary documents — this takes hours. It produces ambiguity and discomfort. It often leads to the conclusion that both sides have some valid points, which satisfies nobody and gets zero engagement on any platform.

Meanwhile, a five-second hot take confirming what you already believe delivers a dopamine hit, social validation from your tribe, and a sense of moral clarity. The information economy has made critical thinking the equivalent of walking to work in a city designed for cars — technically possible, practically irrational.

What This Means for Democracy

If political decisions are driven by emotion, belonging, and narrative rather than evaluation of policy and competence, then the traditional democratic toolkit — debates, manifestos, investigative journalism, fact-checking — is fighting the wrong war. It's like bringing a spreadsheet to a religious revival.

The question isn't how to make people more rational. It's how to build democratic institutions that produce legitimate outcomes even when most participants are operating emotionally. Citizens' assemblies, for instance, work precisely because they create the conditions for rational deliberation — removing participants from their echo chambers, providing balanced expert input, and giving them time. The challenge is scaling that.
